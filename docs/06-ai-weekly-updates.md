# Weekly Updates AI Can Draft for You

Ten minutes on Sunday, one honest letter, every relative who matters informed. This chapter
gives you copy-paste prompts that turn your notes into kind, clear updates using an AI
assistant — so you stop being the switchboard operator and the novelist at the same time.

> **Coordination help, never medical advice.** AI drafts letters; it does not interpret
> symptoms, suggest treatments, or predict outcomes. Anything with clinical content comes
> from the care team's own words, which you are quoting. Anything clinical-sounding the AI
> invents gets deleted. And always double-check names, dates, and facts before sending.

## The three rules of family updates

1. **One channel, one letter.** One weekly letter goes to the whole list (email is great;
   the letter can be printed and mailed). Everyone gets the same facts at the same time.
   No hearsay subcommittees.
2. **Facts and feelings stay labeled.** "Dad walked to the mailbox with the cane on Tuesday"
   is a fact. "It felt like a good week" is a feeling. Keep them next to each other but
   clearly distinct — AI does the formatting, you do the truth.
3. **No medical details in group chats, and never send what the care team told you in
   confidence without asking.** Print and portability are your friends when in doubt.

## What to feed the AI (and what not to)

Do feed it: your short scratch notes ("Tue: cardiology appt, BP machine readings fine, next
appt Sept 14"; "Mom pleased with new walker";) and your one-line mood. Do not feed it:
names-on-paper details beyond what is needed (use first names or initials), full medication
lists, document scans, insurance numbers, or anything you would not put on a postcard.
The AI assistant you use locally or via your chosen provider varies in where text is stored;
treat anything sensitive as the stuff to paraphrase or leave out.

## Prompt 1: the weekly care update (the workhorse)

Copy this into your AI assistant, replacing the bracketed parts:

```text
You are helping me write a warm, plain-English family update letter. Write it in my voice:
simple sentences, no medical jargon, no drama, honest about both progress and setbacks.

Here are my messy notes from this week (may be short; expand nothing, invent nothing):
[PASTE YOUR NOTES]

Write:
1. A 2-4 sentence headline: the single most important thing that happened.
2. Three to six bullet points of facts from my notes, one line each, dated where I gave dates.
3. One short paragraph starting "What we don't know yet:" — open questions and pending items.
4. One closing line asking for something specific and easy, like a card, a call on her
   good hour (mornings), or nothing this week.
5. Subject line, under ten words, informative.

Rules: never diagnose, never name medications unless my notes do, never predict outcomes,
never sound alarmist or falsely cheerful. If my notes are too thin for a decent letter,
say so in one sentence and give me three questions to answer to fill the gaps.
```

## Prompt 2: the "hard week" letter

For the weeks you dread writing. The output is still honest, but kinder to the reader and
to you:

```text
Using the notes below, write my family update for a week I'd describe as hard. Name that
this was a harder week without catastrophizing. Keep the structure from before (headline,
bullets, what we don't know, a small concrete ask) but the closing line should thank the
family for something specific they did, not ask for more. My notes:
[PASTE YOUR NOTES]
```

## Prompt 3: the thank-you note (ten seconds, huge returns)

```text
Write a 3-sentence thank-you note from [MY NAME] to [WHO], who [WHAT THEY DID: e.g., drove
Mom to cardiology on Tuesday and fixed the shower grab bar]. Warm, plain, no flowery
metaphors, no promises of future visits I may not keep.
```

## Prompt 4: turning a rambling call into notes

After a doctor call or sibling phone call, dump your voice-note transcript or bullet
fragments in and get it tidy:

```text
Turn these fragments into (a) a dated checklist of what happened and (b) a to-do list with
owners and dates. Flag anything that sounds like a medical decision for the care team in
ALL CAPS so I remember it isn't my call. Fragments:
[PASTE YOUR FRAGMENTS]
```

## The workflow that makes it stick

1. **Keep a running scratch note all week** — the thesis of this whole guide: notes beat
   memory. Three lines a day is enough. April's letters quietly write themselves.
2. **Sunday, ten minutes:** paste notes into Prompt 1, read the draft, **fix the errors**,
   add the one human line only you can write ("I miss her laugh"), send.
3. **File the sent letter** in your doctor-log binder. Six months of letters is a timeline
   sons, daughters, and caseworkers read like a chart — and it makes
   [doctor visits](04-doctor-logs.md) dramatically easier.

## Privacy gut-check before hitting send

Masses of relatives plus email plus CC lines get messy. Two habits: use **BCC** (or a
mailing-list field in your contact tool) so nobody sees the whole list's addresses; and
before sending, ask: *"would I be fine if this letter were forwarded to the person it
discusses?"* If the letter discusses a person in the third person, imagine them reading it.
Better letters come from that question — usually kinder, always more useful.

*This chapter is about writing family letters with AI assistance. Letters are coordination,
not diagnosis; never medical advice, and the humans in the loop — you, your loved one, and
the care team — remain the authors and the deciders.*
