# Splitting Tasks Without Feuds

Care needs often arrive without an org chart, and families get in trouble when they assume
the org chart instead of writing one. This chapter is the org chart: **who does what, when,
and how you will know** — plus the three conversations that prevent 90% of sibling conflicts.

> **Coordination help, never medical advice.** This chapter is family logistics. Medically
> relevant judgments — how much care someone needs, what visiting hours to accept, what kind
> of facility or level of care is right — come from your loved one and their care team.
> Siblings coordinate the logistics around those facts.

## The three conversations (have them in this order)

1. **The inventory.** Name every task: driving to appointments, refills, bills, laundry
   versus visits, meds management, contractor calls, "visit and sit with them" time, paperwork.
   Include the invisible ones — remembering that the renter's insurance renews in April.
2. **The capacity check.** Everyone states honestly what they *can* offer: time, money, or
   neither. "I handle three Saturday mornings a month" is a real yes. "I can't do anything
   but I'll pay for a cleaning service" is a real yes. "I'll do whatever is needed" is where
   feuds are born, because it means waiting to be asked.
3. **The agreement.** Write it down with names, tasks, and dates. Not a contract — an
   anchor. Memories drift; the paper does not.

## The golden rules of family delegation

- **Nobody owns the cousin-announcer job.** The person who does the task explains the task.
  Others may help but do not adjudicate someone else's work from the outside.
- **Every task has a backup name.** When the primary sibling gets the flu, the plan is
  already on paper.
- **Money talk is a logistics talk.** If someone contributes money instead of hours, write
  down what those funds are *for*, or it turns into a scorecard.
- **The respected elder's preference rules.** Whatever the aging person wants — especially
  if they can still say it — beats everyone's convenience. Coordinate *around* their
  choices, not over them.
- **If everyone lives far away, buy services first, plan visits second.** Long-distance
  systems (meal delivery, transportation services, a paid weekly helper, yard service) do
  the daily work and free visits to be about the person.

## Using the task-split worksheet

One task = one row: task, primary person, backup, frequency, costs involved, and notes.
Work top-down from the inventory conversation. Anything with **money** attached (bills,
reimbursements) gets a second column: how receipts will be shared. A shared folder for
receipts (or one envelope of paper receipts, mailed monthly) keeps money talk factual,
which keeps it feeling like accounting instead of accusation.

## The "what if we disagree" clauses

Before you need them, agree on these three:

- **The escalation clause.** Disagreements get a 24-hour cooling-off, then a 20-minute call
  with all active siblings, agenda: one topic, timeboxed. If you still disagree, the person
  receiving care decides; if they cannot, the designated **agent** in their
  [health-care and financial documents](02-document-grab-list.md) decides, and everyone else
  supports that call.
- **No hostage outcomes.** "Fine, then *you* drive them every week" is a threat and it
  poisons a good plan. Threats during a crisis are not parenting, even if the family calls
  it that.
- **Grace clause.** Anyone may say "I was wrong" or "I can't do this anymore" without
  penalty, and another sibling will take up the task for two weeks. People who are allowed
  to fail stay honest, and honest families finish hard roads.

## The group chat: use it like a bus schedule, not a courtroom

Great for: "who has Saturday, the pharmacy needs a pickup person added, visit moved to 2."
Terrible for: diagnosing mom's sadness, relitigating the inheritance, forwarding
medical updates. Keep the chat at logistics level and keep the emotional level for the
phone — spoken words dissipate; screenshots stay. When one sibling goes quiet instead of
saying "I can't," that is your cue to call them directly.

## Stepmother/grandparent household patterns (quick variants)

- Household input without legal decision authority: offer help, ask what is wanted, and
  remember charm works better than authority (you need no legal status to drive to an
  appointment or to bring a casserole).
- The "huge house, far away, no siblings" case: consider a local professional as the
  daily point of contact (a geriatric care manager) even for a trial month; ask the
  [Area Agency on Aging](01-first-calls-checklist.md) for referrals and compare references.
- The "all children still live within 15 minutes" case: you are rich in hands; your fight
  is scheduling overlap. One shared printed calendar on that fridge beats seventeen apps.

*This chapter organizes the family side of care. It is coordination help, never medical,
legal, or financial advice — clinical, legal, and money decisions have professionals of
their own.*
