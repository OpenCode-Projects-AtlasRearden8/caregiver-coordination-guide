# Medical Logs: Medications

If a nurse in the emergency room asks *"what medications are they on, and when was the last
dose?"* and you can answer from a sheet of paper in thirty seconds, you will watch faces
relax. This chapter teaches you how to keep the caregiver med log — a **record of drugs,
doses, schedules, and changes** — so you can always answer. Fill the blank worksheet from the
[Worksheets index](08-worksheets.md); keep it on a clipboard near the meds.

> **Coordination help, never medical advice.** This chapter teaches record-keeping only.
> It does not tell you what any medication is for, whether a dose is right, whether two drugs
> interact, or whether to skip or split any dose. Those questions belong to your loved one's
> prescriber or pharmacist — and "I'll check my log and ask them" is a great answer.

## The rules that keep logs honest

1. **One log, one home.** All pills live in one place and all changes get written on one
   log. If a sibling or nurse adds a note, it gets copied into the master log that same day.
2. **Write changes on the day they happen.** Any dose change, any new prescription, any
   stopped pill — new line, dated today.
3. **Log the exact dose as written on the label.** "Twice a day" is a reading of the label.
   Copy the label words plus the pharmacy instructions: *"1 tablet by mouth twice daily
   with food"* goes verbatim.
4. **Bring the log to every appointment and every hospital admission.** Doctors fix lists
   in seconds when they can see them, and you will spot a duplicate drug a screen may not.

## What each column of the worksheet is for

| Column | Write here | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Med name | Both brand and generic, label spelling | *lipitor (atorvastatin)* |
| Dose | Number and unit, label spelling | 10 mg |
| How / When | The label instructions, verbatim | 1 tablet by mouth at bedtime |
| Why (optional) | Only what the label or doctor said | "for cholesterol" |
| Prescriber | Name and practice | Dr. A. Rivera — Main St. Clinic |
| Started / Stopped | Date it began, and date + why if stopped | started 2026-03-01; stopped 2026-06-19 (doctor) |

The **Why (optional)** column is the most commonly skipped and the most useful three months
later. If the label does not say why, ask the pharmacist — again, that is a records question.

## The daily check-off page

Alongside the master log, the worksheet includes a **daily check-off grid**: one row per
scheduled time of day, with spaces to note whether each dose was taken. Check marks are fine;
a small "x" or "late" or "vomit" style note when something is off is even better. At the end
of the week, glance at the pattern. Three late doses in one family day usually means the
system needs fixing, not the person.

Fill-in-friendly habits:

- Keep the pencil attached to the clipboard with string. Friction is the enemy.
- When someone refuses or misses a dose, record **what happened, not why** — then ask the
  prescriber the **clinical** questions. "Missed Tuesday and Wednesday" is your sentence.
- Refill dates go in the margin. A refill due the day before travel is the classic near-miss.

## The brown-bag review (once a year, or after any hospital stay)

Once a year — and right after every hospital discharge — put **every** pill bottle, vitamin,
herbal tea, and supplement into a bag and take it to the pharmacist. Ask them to compare the
bag with your master log, flag duplicates and expired items, and tell you what no longer
matches. This is an inventory of *their records*; the pharmacist will tell you to take
clinical questions back to the prescriber, which is exactly right.

## When the log saves the day (true in composite, from caregiver reports)

- Two prescribers prescribing similar-strength drugs for overlapping problems; the log made
  the duplication visible to the next doctor.
- "They took a blue pill this morning, I don't remember which one" written on a full med log
  let the ER pharmacist identify the pill by description in minutes.
- A hospital sent a family home with instructions repeating a drug that had been stopped
  months earlier; the log kept the family confident enough to double-check and ask.

## Copy your log to a backup

Photograph each page once a month into a password-protected place only *you* control, so a
sobbing ER visit or a lost clipboard never erases three weeks of truth. Never send photos of
medications or IDs through group chats or email; use your own secure storage.

*This chapter is a record-keeping habit. It is coordination help, never medical advice —
the prescriber and pharmacist decide anything about the actual medicines.*
